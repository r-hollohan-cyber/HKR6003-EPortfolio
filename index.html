Welcome to my learning ePortfolio for HKR 6003. This platform documents my weekly critical reflections and creative artifacts analyzing health promotion theories across Canada. Throughout this collection, I critically examine individualistic health models by connecting course theory directly to social, economic, and infrastructure realities within the Cape Breton Regional Municipality (CBRM).

Part I: Critical Reflections & Artifacts

Week 1: Rethinking Health Promotion in the CBRM

Weekly Artifact (Canva): View Week 1 Visual Artifact (Replace link with your Canva public view link or embed code)

Before starting this course, I honestly thought health promotion was mostly about education. I figured we just needed to give people the right information so they could make better choices. But reading about the social determinants of health made me reconsider how useful that information really is if someone does not have the resources to act on it.

It made me think a lot about communities around here in the CBRM. It is easy to tell people in River Ryan, New Waterford, or Glace Bay that they need to eat more fresh vegetables to prevent disease. But that advice ignores high grocery prices and a local transit system where taking the bus to a major grocery store can turn a simple errand into a multi-hour hassle if you do not own a car.

This made me question whether some of our local health programs put too much responsibility on individuals. People face major barriers they cannot control. If we just focus on the medical model and warn people about potential diseases, we ignore the root of the problem. I am still trying to figure out how we can promote healthy habits at an individual level without ignoring those larger systemic issues in our community.

Week 2: Motivation Isn't Everything

Weekly Artifact (Canva): View Week 2 Visual Artifact (Replace link with your Canva public view link or embed code)

Reading about Robert Crawford’s (1980) concept of healthism challenged how I view personal responsibility. It made me realize how often health is discussed in our culture as though people have total control over their outcomes.

I do not think individual behaviour models like the Health Belief Model are useless. Understanding personal risk and finding motivation obviously matter, as the study by Kim et al. (2012) showed. The problem, as I see it, is assuming motivation is enough on its own. Knowing the risks of a disease does not give someone the extra income needed to join a local recreation program or buy healthier food.

Quennerstedt and colleagues (2010) point out the difference between just teaching health rules and actually understanding how young people experience health in their daily lives. That distinction resonated with me. It showed how quickly health interventions can turn into victim-blaming if someone does not follow a program, when they really just lacked the environment to succeed. I am still unsure about how health promoters can encourage individual responsibility without turning that responsibility into blame. That seems like one of the harder balances to strike in this field.

Week 3: Unlearning the "Healthy Body"

Weekly Artifact (Canva): View Week 3 Visual Artifact (Replace link with your Canva public view link or embed code)

I realized while reading this week's materials that I have often associated looking fit with actually being healthy, even though those two things are not necessarily the same.

The article by Webb and colleagues (2008) on the cult of the body in physical education made me rethink that assumption. The expectation for PE teachers and students to look a certain way to be considered healthy role models creates an unnecessary barrier (Webb et al., 2008). Judging fitness by appearance can easily push kids away from physical activity altogether.

What struck me most was Oliver’s (2006) discussion of obesity discourses. My interpretation of Oliver's (2006) argument is that framing weight strictly as a disease or an epidemic can end up serving the financial and political interests of the weight-loss industry more than public health itself. That shift in perspective was eye-opening because it moves the focus away from social conditions, like the high child poverty rates we see documented in Cape Breton, and puts all the attention back on individual bodies.

Instead of focusing so heavily on weight and physical appearance, health promotion should focus on making recreation inclusive and supportive of overall well-being. But I am left wondering how we actually shift public perception when the image of the healthy body is so deeply ingrained in our culture and school systems.
