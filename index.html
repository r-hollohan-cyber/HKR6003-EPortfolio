Week 1 Entry Unit 1: Foundations

Week 1 Reflection: Policy Gaps and the Reality of Teaching PE in the CBRM

The readings this week made me think more about something I see in my own teaching: how much responsibility we place on individuals to be healthy without always looking at what makes healthy choices possible in the first place. Hancock (2011) points out that Canada hasn't actually shifted resources into prevention, despite all the talk about health promotion. Much of the focus remains on treating illness rather than preventing it. Raphael et al. (2020) make a similar point about how health is often understood mainly through personal choices such as diet and exercise, without enough attention to the conditions people are living in.

As a PE teacher here in Cape Breton, I see some of these challenges in my own school community. PE can teach movement skills and encourage students to be active, but that doesn't mean every student has the same opportunity to be active outside of school. Many families are dealing with real financial strain. Kids show up in shoes that are falling apart. Families can't always afford gym clothes or water bottles. In winter, when we want to take classes outside, not every student has proper winter gear. When we organize school sports like soccer, games happen right after school in communities that are far away with no bus service, so families can't always get their kids there.

Our breakfast and lunch programs are one example of how the school can help address some of these barriers. Knowing that students can get something to eat at school matters, especially when families are under financial pressure. It also makes me question how effective standard health-promotion messaging can be when students don't have the same resources or opportunities outside of school.

The video transcript also introduced Eisner's idea of the null curriculum. What stood out to me was that what schools leave out can matter just as much as what they formally teach. In PE and health, we can spend a lot of time talking about being active and making healthy choices without giving much attention to whether students have the resources to follow through on those messages. When we talk about healthy living without recognizing people's financial situations, we can make it sound like being unable to make healthy choices is simply a personal failing.

Telling students what they should do is only part of health promotion. There also needs to be time, funding, and real support to address the barriers families are facing. Even something as simple as organizing a sports activity or an opportunity outside school can involve transportation, equipment, scheduling, and whether families are able to get there. Teachers are already doing a lot, so this kind of work needs support beyond the classroom.

I created a Canva graphic this week that maps out our provincial PE outcomes alongside the social determinants of health. I wanted to look more closely at where the curriculum connects with real health factors and where there may be gaps between what we teach and the realities students experience in our school community.

Week 2 Entry Unit 1: Foundations

Week 2 Reflection: What Messages Are We Actually Sending in PE?

There is a clear gap between what we teach students about being healthy and what they actually have access to outside of school, a reality I see regularly as a physical education teacher in rural schools. While we routinely encourage students to exercise, eat well, and get enough rest, we rarely ask whether they have the resources to do so. Raphael et al. (2020) point out that health is often pushed as a personal responsibility without considering whether a person has the resources to follow the advice. For example, I cannot expect my students to practice sport skills at home when they do not have access to basic equipment; many of my students had never even touched a football before my class. When I brought strider bikes to my school, it was the first time on a bike for many of them, and they absolutely loved it. Klein (2010) points out that people often judge someone's character based on their fitness, assuming that being active is just a matter of hard work. In PE class, it is easy for classmates or teachers to think a student is just being lazy when the truth is they have never had the chance to learn.

I think about this a lot in my own teaching, especially when I remember one specific student who lived and breathed soccer at school. He came from a large family without much money; his clothes were well-used and his shoes were falling apart. Even though he played every chance he got during free time, joining the after-school team or a community league wasn’t an option for him because of travel issues. While many schools I have worked at are willing to help with registration fees or equipment, getting students to and from extracurricular activities becomes the main barrier. This connects to intersectionality, which is something I'm still trying to fully understand. Crenshaw (1989) and Heard et al. (2020) talk about how someone's experience is shaped by a bunch of overlapping things rather than just one single barrier. If a student wants to play soccer, it isn't just about paying the registration fee. It's the ride to practice, the cost of gear, and whether they feel like they’ll fit in, all making participation more difficult.

And then there's what happens inside the gym itself. I have to actively catch myself to ensure I don't show accidental favoritism toward the naturally athletic or highly social students. I've noticed that when a gym environment heavily rewards natural ability, other students start checking out because they figure PE isn't really for them. Eisner's idea of the null curriculum, which our course brought up, stuck with me because it makes sense that what schools don't teach matters just as much as what they do teach (HKR 6003, 2015). In PE, we can say all the right things about being active and trying your best, but students are also learning other messages from what they see happening around them, whether those messages are intentional or not.

I see these barriers in my own classes when a student sits out during an activity. I try not to make assumptions about why they aren't participating because the reasons are often surprising, ranging from a grieving pet to a parent working away from home, which really highlights how life outside of school impacts what a student can handle in the class (Raphael et al., 2020). Along with personal stress, many students are not getting much free play outside of school. They tell me they spend most of their time on devices, especially on snow days, or in organized sports, so they rarely get to practice physical skills without supervision (Gray, 2011). Given this, PE class is often the only place they are expected to try new physical movements, making it easy to see why they might hold back in front of their peers. Rather than focusing on just sports, I include simple activities like Grounders, Capture the Flag, or Four Square. This teaches them skills they can use outside of PE (Baek et al., 2024). When children play without adults directly involved, they learn to work through conflicts and make quick decisions (Gray, 2011). Many students, like my daughter, are not interested in joining a competitive sports team, but giving them simple games they can organize with friends gives them a practical way to stay active in their own neighbourhoods.

I have also been rethinking how behavioral change models actually apply to these gym classes. While I was making an ePortfolio mind map this week to break down the core behavior change theories, I realized that I used to design my lessons based mostly on raw physical skills. I was completely missing whether a student actually felt comfortable participating. A student might have the coordination to complete most tasks, but as the Health Belief Model suggests, they will still sit out if the perceived barrier of messing up in front of their peers outweighs the perceived benefit of the game. To synthesize these behavioral models, I created a visual flowchart (Figure 2) illustrating how concepts from the Transtheoretical Model, the Theory of Planned Behavior, and Social Cognitive Theory directly inform how I need to structure my gym environment. Thinking about these factors together showed me that physical education is less about grading athletic skill and more about creating supportive environments where students actually intend to move (Klein, 2010).

As Thomas King argues, if we want a different message about health, we need to tell a different story (HKR 6003, 2015). In my class, this means shifting the focus away from athletic ability and who can run the fastest. Being good at a sport does not tell me if a student is going to stay active outside of school. Instead of just rewarding athleticism, my goal is to help them find physical activities that fit their actual lives, something they can just go outside and do with their friends.

Week 3 Entry Unit 1: Foundations

Week 3 Reflection: Beliefs, Barriers, and What Makes Health Promotion Realistic

Kim et al. (2012) surveyed university students and found that the perceived benefits of eating healthy food, along with the perceived barriers to getting it, significantly shaped what students intended to eat. I see this play out when I pack my daughter’s lunches. Cucumbers, peeled oranges, carrots, and apples used to go to school and come back home more often than not, while crackers, bread, and fruit leather never lasted. Neither of us lacked knowledge about what was healthy; she was a child choosing what she liked, and I was a parent tired of wasting food. I worry far less now that she eats the meals provided through the Nova Scotia School Lunch Program (n.d.), and I'm comfortable with the occasional pizza pop, partly because I grew up on Kraft Dinner, hot dogs, and bologna with a proper meal always alongside them. Among other families I see a different problem, since many students skip the school lunch and their parents end up buying extra lunches or working out how to keep food hot or cold all day. Kim et al. (2012) focus on personal beliefs, while my examples are all about practical hurdles like money and logistics. It feels like a stretch to take a model tested on university students and apply it to children and families. Jose et al. (2021) used this same model in Kerala, India, and found that the community's health beliefs were influenced by the government's healthcare system. Looking at that alongside my own examples makes me question how useful an individual-focused model is when families are up against shared barriers they can't control.

As a teacher, one of the clearest barriers I have seen involved a student that staff advised me against putting on the track team, even though he was my fastest runner. When he suddenly stopped showing up to track meets, his teacher eventually found out it was simply because his family didn't have a vehicle to get him there. It makes me think about how Kim et al. (2012) put so much weight on perceived barriers in their model, but for this student, the barrier wasn't just in his head, it was a real transportation issue that completely vanished once we organized a ride, and he actually ended up winning a medal. You see that same heavy reliance on individual adults over at my daughter's school, where the basketball team almost folded this year just because nobody was available to coach. I keep going back and forth on whether I should volunteer to take it on, but honestly, having zero basketball coaching experience while trying to juggle two master's courses makes me pretty hesitant to commit.

I also saw this from the patient's side during my own rehabilitation. I broke my leg while coaching track and field, demonstrating hurdles at full speed, and I now demonstrate loosely and tell students to do their best while staying safe. Physiotherapy rebuilt my strength and I still use some of those exercises, while osteopathic care worked well for me because the treatment happened during the appointment rather than adding more homework. Hill (2003) examines where complementary and alternative medicine fits within different models of health promotion, and I would probably place my experience closer to the medical model, since I was relying heavily on practitioners to address a physical problem rather than taking on all of the behaviour change myself. Even so, those appointments were the first thing I dropped once teaching and my master's coursework filled my schedule, so a treatment I valued still lost out to everything else. Students face the same pressure when practices, rides, and fees compete with the rest of family life, and an activity that is valuable on paper can still be the first thing to go.

As an uncertain young player I gravitated toward defence in hockey, and when I started playing again after minor hockey I regretted not trying more positions, which is why I encourage young athletes to try several roles before deciding what kind of player they are. A student who never tries a new position narrows their own options, but a student who never gets the chance to join a team has those options narrowed by circumstance. That second situation is where I see a connection to the HIPP Collaborative's focus on inclusion and collaboration (Memorial University, n.d.), including its emphasis on partnerships between the university and the community. Programs like the Capers Elementary League, which connects local children with Cape Breton University soccer players (Soccer Cape Breton, n.d.), show what that can look like, and they spread the load so one parent or teacher isn't carrying a program alone. I'm not sure yet whether something similar could work at my school, but it's the question I keep returning to.

Week 4 Entry Unit 2: Models & Critiques

Week 4 Reflection: Healthism and Physical Education

Crawford (1980) describes healthism as the idea that personal health is one of the main ways people pursue well-being, mostly by changing their lifestyle. He points out that this puts both the cause of health problems and the fix on the individual, even when the problems are tied to things outside the person. Clark (2018) followed girls aged 10 to 13 in the United Kingdom and found that healthism shaped how they took part in sports, including pressure to be slender and a habit of ranking each other’s bodies. When I read the two together, I started asking myself where my own teaching puts the responsibility on the student and where it takes into account what is going on outside the gym.

Teaching in a multicultural school has shown me how much of a student's health depends on things other than effort. I have taught students who were fasting during Ramadan and students who had low iron because of genetics or diet, and in both cases tiredness could easily have been read as a lack of motivation. Several fasting students told me they felt faint, so I reminded them to hydrate outside of fasting hours and encouraged them to adjust their effort rather than trying to set personal bests. I kept a close eye on them and wanted to make sure no one felt they had to hide in the locker room because they were embarrassed. Crawford (1980) describes how, in the healthist view, failing to improve one's health gets attributed to personal habits or a lack of will, and that people with more resources find individual change easier. My students' situations are a reminder that effort is only part of the picture.

The timed run I helped organize at a middle school is where I found these readings hardest to apply, because I enjoyed it and think it was worthwhile. Everything in my classes is voluntary, although students who choose not to take part do not receive a mark, and students who ran could walk if they preferred. The run was held every year, so students could compare their times with those of family, friends, and neighbours who had run before them and with their own times across the three years at the school. I believe students need to feel comfortable challenging themselves and competing, and I learned a lot about them when they pushed themselves as hard as they could. Some students did ask to be excused, usually because of a recent leg injury or an illness, although they were generally fine once the run was over. Some students also commented on the bodies and abilities of others, which we addressed by speaking with everyone involved and, when the comments were hurtful, by filing an incident report and calling both sets of parents. Clark (2018) describes students bringing notes from home to avoid physical education and body comments coming from both girls and boys, which is similar to what I saw. Her findings make me more aware that comparing times is easier for confident students, even though I still think the run was a simple tradition that most students enjoyed.

My own experience with body measurement adds to this. In high school we did the BMI test and a skinfold test, and I was classified as obese, which would still be the case today, even though I consider myself to be in good shape. Quennerstedt et al. (2010) note that BMI testing is used in some countries as a form of health surveillance of children, and they argue that tying education to weight goals holds each student accountable for their body and assumes one shared goal for everyone. My experience makes me think that if students are tested, they need to be taught what a test measures and what it cannot tell them.

Quennerstedt et al. (2010) also suggest moving away from teaching young people to be healthy and toward understanding how they learn health, and they write that it is just as important to understand why young people learn not to take part in physical activity. In my classes, most students who sit out tell me the activities are boring, that they are not interested, and that I should do something fun. That is a different reason from lack of effort or concern about their health, and it is one I can act on. It is part of why I like to expose students to a variety of athletic challenges, since you never know what will spark a student's interest. Last year I taught the high jump to my elementary classes and needed to choose three male and three female students for an elementary track and field meet. Each student could sign up for only two events, so I first asked students who had done well and were not in too many events. That approach favours students who are already confident, so when students who do not usually take part in sports were interested, I made sure to include at least a few of them, and some of those students became very focused and kept asking to try again.

Week 5 Entry Unit 3: Contextual Perspectives

Week 5 Reflection: Deconstructing the “Healthy Body” in the Gym

Webb et al. (2008) describe perceived expectations in physical education for teachers to be appropriate, fit, healthy, and skilful role models and for students to display a slim body that is equated with fitness and health. A coworker I considered a mentor, who was overweight, was criticized by administrators, colleagues, and students for his ability to teach physical education, which matches what the authors describe. One of their Australian participants told her classes that not all physical education teachers are thin, tall, and fast, yet she also said it would be good if she were ten kilograms lighter (Webb et al., 2008), and I recognize some of that tension in myself. I like to stay relatively fit and I benefit from the same expectation, so I am not sure how much of my routine comes from wanting to model an active life and how much comes from wanting to look the part. At the elementary level I do not use fitness testing, and I demonstrate activities, encourage children to challenge themselves, and join in, which gives me exercise while leading by example. The authors also note that without test results the temptation is to judge fitness and health from body shape (Webb et al., 2008), which makes me want to pay closer attention to the assumptions I make when I look at the bodies in my gym.

The same article focuses on teachers’ surveillance of student bodies, which the authors link to a “cult of the body” that can contribute to problems such as eating disorders and exercise addiction (Webb et al., 2008). In my middle school classes the comments came from classmates instead of from teachers. I let students choose their own groups and partners so they would feel comfortable exercising, but some who participated and tried their best received comments about their weight, and others protested having them on their teams. I handled these situations privately to avoid drawing attention to the student, and when comments were hurtful I sometimes sent the speaker to the office or had them sit out. Fitzpatrick (2019) points out that physical education is a class where fat-phobic comments and assumptions persist, and that students judged to be overweight often feel marginalized, excluded, and judged. Giving students a choice of groups was meant to help them feel comfortable, yet it also gave some of them the chance to exclude others, so I am still working out how to balance choice with protection.

The unit video points out that you cannot judge how healthy someone is by looking at them, since a slim person may be that way because of genetics, smoking, or an eating disorder, while a person with a larger body may be metabolically healthy (Memorial University, n.d.). In my classes, thinness has not predicted how much a student participates, and I have taught every type of student. Oliver (2006) argues that, given inconclusive evidence and the absence of a safe and effective weight loss regimen, calling weight an epidemic disease is likely to cause more harm than good, and he links the label to financial and political incentives in the weight loss industry, the medical profession, and public health agencies. He does not claim that weight never matters, since he allows that very high body fat may interfere with movement and joint health for some people, and I think that is a fair limit to keep in mind. Oliver writes about the United States, so I am cautious about applying his argument directly to Canadian schools, although he also reports that weight gain was concentrated among poorer populations and in largely rural, poor states (Oliver, 2006), which makes me wonder how weight messages reach students in a rural community like mine.

The video also reports that an estimated 88% of Canadian children and youth, and over 95% of adolescent girls, do not get enough physical activity for optimum health, even though most are aware that activity matters (Memorial University, n.d.). My own students complicate that picture, because many are active outside of school in ways that rarely appear in a gym class. Several enjoy fishing or hunting with their parents, and many ride dirt bikes or ATVs, which makes me think rural students may be more active outdoors than those national figures would suggest. I do not know how this kind of activity compares with what the statistics measure, but it clearly involves time outside, balance, and physical effort. I still think electronics are the biggest barrier I see, since on snow days and weekends I often hear students talk about video games and movies instead of outdoor play. Gray (2011), whose review draws mostly on data from the United States, names television and computer games as one explanation for declining outdoor play, but he also points to parents’ safety concerns, more time in school, and a lack of other children to play with, and he reports that most children prefer outdoor play with friends over screens. That fits what I notice more closely than a simple story about electronics, because many of my students seem willing to play outside but cannot gather enough friends for the large games I grew up playing, such as street hockey, basketball, and manhunter.

Fitzpatrick (2019) argues that critical practice is needed more than ever, because physical education remains exclusionary for many students and, building on Hawkins’s argument, the field is shaped by pragmatism, materialism, and health concerns (Hawkins, 2008, as cited in Fitzpatrick, 2019). She also asks why fitness testing is treated as a requirement when such tests measure fitness instead of learning. Among her examples is a teacher named Dan, who built relationships by learning about his students’ hopes, families, and talents, favoured team games over performance-based activities, and avoided competitive displays of skill while joking about himself (Fitzpatrick, 2019). His approach gives me a more concrete model than the unit’s call for body-positive teaching. In my own classes I plan to keep fitness testing out of elementary, add team games such as manhunter that do not depend on body type, and ask students about the fishing, hunting, and riding they already do so that it counts as part of being active. In middle school I will keep handling weight comments privately and will consider assigning some groups myself. Because Gray (2011) separates adult-directed games from free play, I see these games as a way to teach skills and rules that students can later use with friends on their own, not as a replacement for play they organize themselves.
